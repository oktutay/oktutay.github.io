Why I work on AI for security

I came to security the practical way. During my internship at VNPT Cyber Immunity I spent months reproducing the same families of bugs over and over - SQL injection, XSS, CSRF, path traversal, insecure deserialization, broken access control - writing up each one so someone else could reproduce it too. That work teaches you something that no course does: most of the effort is not finding the bug, it is explaining it well enough that a person will act on it.

That is also what pulled me toward AI. A malware classifier that says "malicious, 0.94" is nearly useless to a tier-2 analyst. They need to know which parts of the binary drove the verdict, whether the model has seen anything like it before, and whether its answer still holds now that the malware has evolved. Those are the two problems I keep coming back to: robustness under concept drift, and explanations grounded in the model's own evidence rather than a plausible story generated after the fact. They are the core of LLMal, the paper I worked on this year, and of MalEvo, the platform we built from it.

The other half of my work is making these systems usable. BAIStudio came out of watching non-technical people bounce off AI tooling that assumed they already knew what a pipeline was. Building it taught me that a good interface is not decoration on top of a model - it decides whether the model gets used at all.

I plan to write here about what I learn along the way: notes on graph-based malware analysis, things that surprised me while testing web applications, and the occasional post-mortem on a project that did not work. If any of it is useful to you, or wrong, I would like to hear about it.
